Eye and orbit anatomy (transverse section)

Tap a structure name to highlight it.

The optic nerve sheath is a continuation of the dura. The subarachnoid space around the nerve communicates with the intracranial CSF, so raised ICP distends the sheath, most of all just behind the globe.

Ultrasound appearance and where to measure

  1. A: find the posterior globe where the optic nerve enters (the optic disc).
  2. A to B: go 3 mm straight back along the nerve axis, where the sheath is widest.
  3. At B: measure perpendicular to the nerve (and its vessels), from the outer edge of one hyperechoic sheath wall to the outer edge of the other. Never include the black shadows outside the sheath.
  4. Align first: rock until the nerve is parallel to the beam, fan until the sheath is widest.
  5. Confirm it is the nerve: turn on color Doppler briefly. Red choroidal flow arcs along the back of the globe, and the central retinal artery (red) and vein (blue) appear as a linear red and blue pair in the center of the nerve just behind the disc, the red flashing with each heartbeat. A dark stripe with no flow, especially one that widens with depth, is shadow artifact.
  6. Repeat in the transverse and sagittal planes, scan both eyes, and average (best three).
  7. If the ONSD is 5 to 6 mm, measure the optic disc elevation (ODE).

Technique (rule of 3 x 5)

  1. Prepare: head of bed about 45 degrees. Close the eye, cover it with a large transparent waterproof dressing, push out the air, and apply a generous layer of water-based gel.
  2. Machine: high-frequency linear probe (7.5 to 10 MHz or higher). Use the ophthalmic preset. If there is none, use small parts with output turned to the minimum. Keep MI below 0.23 and TI at or below 1.0; Doppler raises power output, so use it briefly.
  3. Probe: pencil grip, indicator to the patient's right in the transverse plane, very light pressure, and the side of your hand braced on the cheek, nasal bridge or forehead.
  4. Survey near to far: lid, cornea, anterior chamber, iris, lens, vitreous, retina, then the optic nerve behind the globe. Scan both eyes.
  5. Align: rock the probe (often about 15 degrees laterally) or have the patient change gaze until the sheath runs parallel to the beam. An oblique nerve blurs the walls and invites edge artifact.
  6. Confirm with color Doppler: briefly turn on color. Look for the straight red (artery) and blue (vein) line in the center of the nerve, alternating with each heartbeat; this confirms the nerve and shows its true direction. Measure perpendicular to the vessels. Keep Doppler time short (it raises TI).
  7. Measure: from the posterior globe at the disc, go 3 mm straight back along the nerve. At that point, measure the sheath outer wall to outer wall. Repeat in the sagittal plane and average. Take several measurements and average the best three.

Interpreting ONSD

ONSDTeaching framework (NUEM SonoPro)
< 5 mmLikely normal ICP
5 to 6 mmIndeterminate: look for optic disc elevation (papilledema)
> 6 mmSuggests elevated ICP
  • Published cutoffs vary (roughly 4.8 to 5.9 mm). In ICU studies of severe TBI against invasive ICP, the best cutoff was about 5.7 mm (Geeraerts 2007; Soldatos 2008), and ICP above 20 mmHg corresponds to roughly 5.7 to 6.0 mm (ASRA review).
  • Baseline sheath size varies between people: a value above 6 mm is not always pathologic, and below 5 mm does not guarantee normal ICP.
  • One ONSD value can correspond to an ICP range wider than 10 mmHg. Ultrasound gives snapshots, while ICP is dynamic (Geeraerts 2008).
  • ONSD responds within minutes, which makes serial measurement useful; a change of more than 5% between measurements has been treated as an unstable trend.
  • Hypercapnia raises ONSD (via ICP) and it returns toward baseline with hyperventilation. Acute blood pressure swings over 10% can also change it.
  • ONSD is a screening and trending tool. Strategies built only on noninvasive ICP estimates are not recommended in place of invasive monitoring when that is indicated.

Optic disc elevation (papilledema)

  • Measure the height of the disc dome above the surrounding retina.
  • ODE above 0.6 mm: sensitivity 82%, specificity 76% for fundoscopic disc edema. ODE of 1.0 mm or more: sensitivity 73%, specificity 100%.
  • Disc elevation lags ONSD by hours to days, so its absence does not exclude raised ICP. It is most helpful when ONSD is indeterminate.

Pitfalls

  • Lamina cribrosa shadow: the nerve passes through a collagen mesh at the back of the globe that casts a black, tube-like shadow straight down the beam, widening with depth. It is often mislabeled as the nerve; measuring it inflates the ONSD (in one demonstration, 5.8 mm for the artifact vs 3.1 mm for the actual nerve).
  • The nerve curves: its course changes with the angle of insonation. Center it so it runs straight down from the disc, and check with color Doppler that the stripe you measure contains the central retinal artery and vein.
  • Edge artifact: shadows from the lens edges run straight down the image and can be mistaken for the nerve sheath, especially when the nerve is oblique.
  • Too much pressure: distorts the globe and the measurement and is hazardous.
  • Air under the dressing: reverberation and dropout.
  • Unstable hand: brace on the face; fatigue causes drift.
  • High output presets: the eye is thermally sensitive tissue (avascular cornea and lens).
